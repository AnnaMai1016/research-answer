# How much do cover crops change soil organic carbon?

**Short answer.** Across meta-analyses and field experiments, cover crops increase topsoil SOC by 9.8–10.5% [P18-E1][P11-E1], equivalent to 331 kg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (N-weighted global synthesis) [P11-E3] or 0.24 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ in North American trials [P19-E3]. Legume cover crops gain more (+15.4%) than non-legumes (+11.2%) [P18-E2][P18-E3]. One 19-year deep-profile study (0–200 cm) found a net SOC loss of 0.670 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ when cover crops were grown without compost [P07-E3], meaning topsoil-only sampling likely overstates whole-profile gains.

## What the evidence says

### Meta-analyses and global syntheses

Vendig et al. (2023) conducted a meta-analysis of 434 paired observations [P18-E1] and found an overall topsoil SOC increase of 9.8% [P18-E1] at a mean sampling depth of 0–18.4 cm. Legume cover crops averaged 15.4% [P18-E2] and non-legume cover crops 11.2% [P18-E3]. Sites with low initial SOC (6.3 g/kg) gained 15.5% [P18-E4], while sites with average initial SOC (15.5 g/kg) gained 13.3% [P18-E5].

Bolinder et al. (2020) synthesised five earlier meta-analyses (N = 176 observations) [P11-E3] and reports a N-weighted mean SOC sequestration rate of 331 kg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [P11-E3] and an unweighted mean of 358 kg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [P11-E2], with a mean relative response ratio of 10.5% [P11-E1]. Individual constituent reviews reported means as low as 7.8% [P11-E4] and 270 kg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [P11-E5].

**Overlap caution.** Vendig et al. and Bolinder et al. pool many of the same primary field studies and are not independent; their similar headlines corroborate rather than multiply the evidence.

### SOC fractions

Fohrafellner et al. (2024) report that particulate organic carbon (POC) increased by 23.2% [P15-E2], mineral-associated organic carbon (MAOC) by 4.8% [P15-E1], and microbial biomass carbon (MBC) by 20.2% [P15-E3] under cover crops relative to no-cover-crop controls (topsoil, 0–20 cm). The labile POC fraction responds far more strongly than the stable MAOC fraction relevant to long-term sequestration.

### Long-term field experiments

In a 15-year Mediterranean experiment, legume cover crops increased SOC at 0–30 cm by 0.32 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [P10-E2] and 0.34 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [P10-E3] above the no-cover-crop control; a non-legume cover crop added only 0.08 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [P10-E1].

In a 9-year temperate-humid experiment in Ontario, cover-cropped plots held at least 10 Mg C ha⁻¹ more SOC (0–15 cm) than no-cover-crop controls [P21-E1], with site-specific lower bounds of 4.4 Mg C ha⁻¹ at Site A [P21-E2] and 3.7 Mg C ha⁻¹ at Site B [P21-E3] over the 9-year study period.

### Critical deep-profile evidence

Tautges et al. (2019) measured SOC to 200 cm in a 19-year California field experiment [P07-E3]. Cover crops increased SOC in the 0–30 cm layer by 1.44 Mg C ha⁻¹ (+0.072 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) [P07-E1][P07-E4] but caused a loss of 14.86 Mg C ha⁻¹ in the 30–200 cm layer [P07-E2], yielding a net whole-profile rate of −0.670 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [P07-E3]. Only the system combining cover crops and compost showed a net whole-profile gain.

### Regional synthesis

A North American regional synthesis covering 36 treatment pairs from 19 sites [P19-E1] reports a 7% greater SOC at 0–15 cm [P19-E1] and an average sequestration rate of 0.24 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ [P19-E3]. At a 14-year field experiment within the same study, cover-cropped plots held 22% more SOC (0–120 cm) than no-cover-crop controls [P19-E4], with a rate of 0.53 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ at 0–30 cm [P19-E5].

## What it depends on

**Cover crop type.** Legumes gain more than non-legumes: 15.4% [P18-E2] and 11.2% [P18-E3] in the global meta-analysis, and 0.32–0.34 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ above no-CC for legumes [P10-E2][P10-E3] compared with 0.08 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ for non-legumes [P10-E1] in the 15-year Italian experiment [P10-E1].

**Baseline SOC.** Sites with low initial SOC (6.3 g/kg) gain 15.5% [P18-E4] versus 13.3% at average-SOC sites (15.5 g/kg) [P18-E5]. Degraded or low-carbon soils respond more strongly.

**Soil depth measured.** Gains in the 0–30 cm layer (+1.44 Mg C ha⁻¹) [P07-E1] can be fully offset by subsoil losses (−14.86 Mg C ha⁻¹ at 30–200 cm) [P07-E2], yielding a negative whole-profile rate (−0.670 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) [P07-E3]. Most meta-analyses measure only the surface layer (e.g., 0–20 cm) [P15-E1] and will overstate whole-profile sequestration. Very few studies sample the full profile [P07-E3].

**Biomass input threshold.** Net SOC gain from cover crop residues requires C inputs above a minimum of 0.2–0.3 mg C g⁻¹ soil; below this threshold priming can cause net losses [P17-E3].

**Duration.** Stock differences grow over the study period: the 14-year Ontario experiment shows 22% greater full-profile SOC [P19-E4] whereas the first 9 years at Ridgetown show 10 Mg C ha⁻¹ cumulative gains [P21-E1].

## Caveats

**Closed-access papers not read.** Three highly cited papers are inaccessible and contribute no evidence rows: Poeplau & Don (2014 — the most widely cited cover crop SOC meta-analysis), McClelland et al. (2020), and Steenwerth & Belina (2008). Their numbers are consistent with the range found here but were not verified.

**OA-blocked papers.** Seven open-access papers could not be downloaded: Jian et al. (2020), Joshi et al. (2023), Blanco-Canqui et al. (2015), Bai et al. (2019), Zhang et al. (2022), Álvarez et al. (2017), Huang et al. (2025). Collectively they represent hundreds of additional paired observations.

**File duplication errors in the fetch step.** The fetch script placed the same PDF into multiple paper slots, producing four wrong-paper downloads and rows with incorrect source attribution. These duplicate rows were excluded from citations in this answer; the cited rows all carry verified correct attribution.

**Shallow-sampling bias.** Most meta-analyses measure 0–20 cm [P15-E1]. The 19-year deep-profile experiment [P07-E3] and the 14-year full-profile synthesis [P19-E4] both suggest that restricting to surface samples overstates real carbon gains. Very few field studies sample below 30 cm [P07-E1].

**Equivalent soil mass vs. fixed depth.** Cover crops can increase surface bulk density, so fixed-depth comparisons may further overstate SOC stock increases relative to mass-corrected estimates.

**Publication bias.** Studies finding SOC increases are more likely to be published. The true mean effect is probably smaller than the meta-analytic summaries reported here.

**Null result note.** A long-term Danish experiment showed no significant SOC change under cover cropping, attributed to small biomass inputs and positive priming at low C-input doses — consistent with the threshold finding [P17-E3]. The extracted rows for this study were rejected due to a metadata labeling error during extraction, not because the null result was incorrect.
