# Baseline answer (from memory)

Cover crops consistently increase soil organic carbon (SOC), though the magnitude varies with cover crop species, climate, soil type, tillage system, and study duration.

Meta-analyses suggest that cover crops increase SOC concentrations by approximately 0.1–0.5 g C kg-1 soil in surface layers (0–20 cm), which translates to relative increases of roughly 5–15% compared to no-cover-crop controls. In absolute stock terms, several syntheses report mean increases of about 0.1–0.3 Mg C ha-1 yr-1, with some long-term studies showing larger accumulations approaching 0.5 Mg C ha-1 yr-1.

Poeplau and Don (2015) conducted an influential meta-analysis of 139 observations across Europe and found a mean SOC increase of +0.32 Mg C ha-1 yr-1 under cover crops relative to bare fallow or cash-crop-only controls, with an overall mean SOC stock increase of approximately 11% in the 0–22 cm depth layer after a mean duration of 9 years. Legume cover crops and grass-legume mixtures tend to produce slightly larger gains than cereal cover crops alone, partly because of enhanced nitrogen inputs.

Preissel et al. (2015) and subsequent work by Blanco-Canqui et al. (2015) highlight that benefits are greatest in degraded or low-carbon soils, in humid climates, and when cover crops are grown for multiple consecutive years. Deeper soil layers (20–40 cm) show smaller but detectable increases in some studies. Under no-till systems combined with cover crops, SOC gains are generally larger than under conventional tillage.

A global meta-analysis by Xu et al. (2023) reported a mean SOC increase of ~8–12% in the topsoil (0–30 cm) across diverse agroecosystems, with the largest effects in tropical and subtropical regions due to higher biomass inputs.

Overall, cover crops are a reliable but modest SOC-building practice, typically adding 0.1–0.5 Mg C ha-1 yr-1 in surface soils.
