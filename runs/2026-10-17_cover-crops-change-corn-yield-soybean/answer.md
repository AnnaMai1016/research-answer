# How much do cover crops change corn yield and soybean yield?

**Short answer.** For corn, the average effect is small and its direction depends on the cover crop. North American meta-analyses find a pooled corn yield gain of 13% ± 3% [P04-E1] or a non-significant response ratio of 1.03 [P03-E1]. Legumes raise corn yield (RR 1.21) [P01-E1], grasses leave it unchanged (RR 1) [P01-E2] or lower it by 6% [P04-E8], and a satellite study of US Corn Belt farm fields found a 5.5% maize yield loss [P02-E1]. For soybean, most syntheses find no significant effect: RR 1.06 [P03-E2], p = 0.2678 [P04-E2], and −2% in the Argentine Pampas [P09-E3]. Losses of 3.5% appear on US farm fields [P02-E2] and 11.6% in drylands [P08-E1], while Brazilian soybean gains 8.72% [P10-E1].

## What the evidence says

### Corn: meta-analyses

**North America.** In the updated corn meta-analysis by Marcillo and Miguez (2017), corn after a legume winter cover crop yielded RR 1.21 (1.17 to 1.29) relative to no cover crop [P01-E1]. After a mixture it yielded RR 1.13 [P01-E3], and after a grass RR 1 (0.98 to 1.02), which is not different from no cover [P01-E2]. Bourgeois and colleagues (2022; 86 studies, mostly Canada and the USA) found that cover crops increased corn yield by 13% ± 3% overall [P04-E1], or 11% when full-season cover crop systems were excluded [P04-E3]. In the same analysis, legumes raised corn yield by 21% while grasses lowered it by 6% [P04-E7][P04-E8]. Chahal and colleagues (2023) found a smaller, non-significant gain for grain corn (RR = 1.03, n = 326) [P03-E1], and a loss for silage corn (RR = 0.90, n = 41) [P03-E3].

**Other regions.** Corn and maize respond positively in Europe (+12%, n = 152) [P11-E1]. They also respond positively in China after winter legumes (27.2 ± 9.9%) [P12-E2]. In the Argentine Pampas, legumes increased corn yield by about 7% [P09-E1], roughly 0.65 t grain/ha [P09-E4], while non-legumes tended to decrease it by about 8% (not significant) [P09-E2]. In global drylands the maize response was 1.2%, not significant [P08-E2].

**Legumes as a replacement for fertilizer N.** Tonitto and colleagues (2005) compared cover crop systems with bare fallow under recommended N rates. With non-legume cover crops and full fertilizer, yields averaged a 3% decline that was not significant [P07-E1]. When unfertilized corn after a legume was compared with conventionally fertilized corn, corn yields were 12% lower [P07-E2]. So legumes add yield on top of fertilizer but do not fully replace it.

**Overlap caution.** Marcillo and Miguez, Bourgeois and colleagues, Chahal and colleagues, and Tonitto and colleagues all draw mainly on North American field trials, so many of their primary studies are the same. Their estimates (a legume benefit, grasses close to zero) are corroborating, not independent.

### Corn: farm fields and field experiments

**Farm-scale observational evidence.** Deines and colleagues (2022) applied causal forests to satellite yield maps of fields that had cover crops for 3 or more years [P02-E1]. They estimated an average maize yield loss of 5.5% (95% CI 5.1–5.9) [P02-E1]. These fields are predominantly cereal rye on commercial farms, not the legume-heavy trials that dominate the meta-analyses.

**Cereal rye before corn.** In Iowa, a rye cover crop reduced corn grain yield by 6% at the economic optimum N rate, which is 0.79 Mg/ha [P13-E1][P13-E2]. Averaged across N rates, the loss was 0.95 Mg/ha [P13-E3]. In 30% of site-years, the loss was more than 1 Mg/ha [P13-E5]. A second Iowa trial of 12 winter cereal cultivars found that corn after the cover crops yielded 0.86 Mg/ha less than the no-cover check in one year [P25-E2]. The gap was 0.37 Mg/ha in the next year [P25-E3] and 0.31 Mg/ha (not significant) in a third [P25-E4].

**Other trials.** In Tennessee, cover crop type had no significant effect on corn, whose mean yields ranged from 14.1 to 15 Mg/ha across treatments [P22-E3]. In northeastern Italy, silage maize after rye yielded 17.5 Mg/ha against 19.3 Mg/ha after weedy fallow [P27-E1]. After crimson clover it yielded 14.2 against 16.5 Mg/ha [P27-E3]. In Pennsylvania, maize after cereal rye yielded 39% and 43% less than after red clover [P17-E1][P17-E2]. That is a rye-versus-legume contrast, not a cover-versus-no-cover effect.

### Soybean

**Meta-analyses.** In North America, the soybean response was non-significant: RR = 1.06 (n = 109) [P03-E2], p = 0.2678 [P04-E2], and p = 0.0862 when full-season cover crops were excluded [P04-E4]. Soybean yield in the Argentine Pampas was minimally affected (−2%) [P09-E3]. In global drylands it fell by 11.6% (95% CI −19.6 to −2.9) [P08-E1], the only significant negative soybean effect among the meta-analyses. Brazil is the clear positive case: across 826 observations from 53 studies, soybean yield rose by 8.72% (95% CI 6.40–11.15) [P10-E1]. That gain is about 288 kg/ha of additional grain [P10-E2].

**Farm-scale observational evidence.** On US Corn Belt fields, soybean yields were 3.5% lower (95% CI 3.2–3.9) after cover crop adoption [P02-E2].

**Field experiments.** In Iowa, rye had no significant effect on the following soybean crop (4.01 against 4.04 Mg/ha) [P13-E6]. A Pennsylvania trial found no cover crop effect on soybean yield either (mean 3.1 Mg/ha, p = 0.948) [P14-E4]. In Tennessee, a five-species mix increased soybean yield by 6 to 8.5% [P22-E1] (3.5 against 3.2–3.28 Mg/ha) [P22-E2]. In a single Tennessee season, a multispecies mix gave 15% higher soybean yield than the control [P23-E1], whereas cereal rye was lower but not significantly different (3.9 Mg/ha) [P23-E3]. In a 12-year Brazilian no-till experiment, sunn hemp raised soybean yield by an average of 183 kg/ha over 10 seasons compared with fallow and chiseling [P24-E1].

### Context: syntheses that pool all cash crops

Several syntheses do not separate corn and soybean, so they are not used for the headline numbers. Abdalla and colleagues (2019) found a −4% average effect on primary crop yield (n = 154) [P05-E1]. Vendig and colleagues (2023) found +10.9% (95% CI 7.5–14.5) [P06-E1]. Shackelford and colleagues (2019) found, for California and the Mediterranean, +16% after legumes and −7% after non-legumes [P29-E1][P29-E2].

## What it depends on

**Cover crop type (the dominant factor for corn).** Legumes raise corn yield and grasses do not: RR 1.21 against RR 1 [P01-E1][P01-E2], or +21% against −6% [P04-E7][P04-E8]. In the Pampas the same split was +7% against −8% [P09-E1][P09-E2]. Rye-dominated farm fields show losses of 5.5% [P02-E1]. For Brazilian soybean, single-species grasses (16.97%) and legumes (16.06%) both gave large benefits [P10-E7][P10-E8]. That soybean benefit also grew by 2.61% per additional Mg/ha of grass cover crop biomass [P10-E9].

**Nitrogen fertilizer rate.** The legume benefit to corn is largest at low N. It was 30% to 33% at low N fertilizer rates [P01-E4], against only 9% at 100 to 199 kg N/ha [P01-E5]. Likewise it was 32% with no N fertilizer and 22% at 1–60 kg N/ha [P04-E9]. Conversely, grass cover crops cost corn 17% of yield with no fertilizer, a loss not seen above 60 kg N/ha [P04-E10]. Much of the corn benefit from legumes is therefore an N credit that shrinks when corn is fully fertilized.

**Tillage.** Under conventional tillage, corn after legumes yielded 15% more than corn without a cover crop [P01-E6], while under no-till the gain was 30% [P01-E7].

**Termination timing.** Mixtures terminated late (zero to six days before corn) increased corn yield by 30% [P01-E8].

**Duration.** The corn benefit grows with years of cover cropping. It was 10% in the first year [P04-E5] and 25% after 2–5 years [P04-E6].

**What the cover crop replaces, and climate.** In Brazil, soybean gained 10.60% when cover crops replaced fallow [P10-E3], but only 4.77% when they replaced a previous cash crop [P10-E4]. Gains were also larger in the tropical (17.14%) than the subtropical zone (9.01%) [P10-E5][P10-E6]. In drylands, where cover crops compete for water, soybean lost 11.6% [P08-E1].

**Region within North America.** For corn the response ratio was 1.12 and 1.14 in the Southeast and Northeast United States [P01-E9][P01-E10].

## Caveats

**Trials versus farms.** Meta-analyses of research plots tend to show neutral-to-positive effects, while the only farm-scale study shows losses for both crops [P02-E1][P02-E2]. That study is observational: farmers who adopt cover crops may differ from those who do not, although its authors ran a placebo test on pre-adoption yields (rejected here as not a cover crop effect). Research plots also tend to use legumes more often and manage cover crops more carefully than typical farms.

**Different controls.** "No cover crop" means bare fallow in some studies, weedy fallow in others [P27-E1], and a previous cash crop in others [P10-E4]. Effects against a cash-crop control are smaller.

**Ratios, percentages and absolute yields are not pooled here.** The numbers above are reported in the unit each paper used and should not be averaged across studies.

**Papers not read.** Two open-access papers could not be downloaded: Rosa and colleagues (2021, cover crop species and rainfed maize) and Jian and colleagues (2020, a cover crop calculator built on a large global dataset). The landmark Miguez and Bollero (2005) corn meta-analysis was not among the screened papers and is only represented indirectly through its update by Marcillo and Miguez [P01-E1].

**Evidence lost in review.** All eight rows from Acharya and colleagues (2019, an Iowa corn and soybean rotation trial) were rejected because the extracted quotes were stitched together from table cells and could not be matched to the text. The reviewer found the values themselves were correct, so this is a gap in verified evidence, not contrary evidence. Three hand-added papers (Villamil and colleagues 2006, Nkongolo and colleagues 2015, Raut and colleagues 2020) yielded no extractable rows.

**Results the extractor missed.** The reviewer flagged that in the Pennsylvania drought trial no cover crop changed maize yield significantly compared with fallow. It also flagged that the earlier corn and soybean years of the Tennessee multispecies trial were not significant. These are not used as numbers in this answer.
