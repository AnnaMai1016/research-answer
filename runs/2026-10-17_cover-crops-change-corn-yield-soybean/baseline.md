# How much do cover crops change corn and soybean yield?

**Short answer:** In most cases the effect is small, from a few percent lower to roughly neutral. The direction mostly depends on what kind of cover crop is grown and how much nitrogen (N) the cash crop gets.

**Corn.** Meta-analyses show a clear split between legume and grass covers. Miguez and Bollero (2005) found that legume cover crops raised corn yield by about 37% when no N fertilizer was applied, and grass–legume bicultures raised it by about 21%. Grass covers such as cereal rye had no significant effect on average. The legume benefit shrinks as N rate increases and is close to zero at agronomic N rates. Marcillo and Miguez (2017) updated this work and confirmed the pattern: legume and mixed covers increased corn yield, while grass covers were neutral to slightly negative. Tonitto et al. (2006) reported that corn grown on legume-supplied N yielded about 10% less than conventionally fertilized corn, and that non-legume covers caused no yield loss when fertilizer was applied. In Iowa, a cereal rye cover before corn often lowered yield by roughly 5–6% unless extra N was added (Pantoja et al., 2015).

**Soybean.** The effects are smaller. Rye cover before soybean usually has no significant effect, with typical changes of 0 to ±3% (Pantoja et al., 2015).

**Large-scale and global evidence.** Deines et al. (2023) used satellite data from about 90,000 US Midwest fields. Fields that adopted cover crops had average yield losses of about 5.5% for corn and 3.5% for soybean. Abdalla et al. (2019) found that, globally, cover crops reduced the following cash crop's yield by about 4% on average. Legume covers tended to increase yield, and non-legumes decreased it. Qin et al. (2021) used process modelling and found Midwest yield changes generally within ±5%. Farmer surveys such as SARE/CTIC report small gains (about +2–3%), but these are not controlled comparisons.

**Bottom line:** For corn, expect roughly −6% to +5% at full N rates, and up to +20–40% from legumes at low N. For soybean, expect about −4% to +3%.
